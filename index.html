Field notes

1 · What a pose is

A qubit is the quantum version of a bit — instead of being plainly 0 or 1, it holds an amplitude (a signed number whose square is a probability) for each. "Striking a pose" is a measurement: it forces each qubit to answer up (+1) or down (−1), along whatever direction its dial points. The dial matters because qubits, unlike coins, can be asked the question from any angle.

2 · Spooky agreement

Set both dials to the same angle — any angle — and the arms always match, even though each arm alone is a perfect 50/50 coin flip. Entanglement means the two qubits don't have separate answers written down anywhere; they share one joint state, so the randomness itself is shared.

3 · Couldn't it be a script?

Maybe the dancers just agreed on answers beforehand — a hidden script (physicists say local hidden variables). A script explains same-angle agreement fine. But turn the dials apart and watch the chart: scripts produce correlation that fades in a straight line, while entanglement fades as a cosine curve. At Δ = 45° the gap is undeniable.

4 · The number that settles it

The Bell score (CHSH) adds up correlations from four dial settings. John Bell proved in 1964 that every possible script tops out at 2; entangled qubits reach 2√2 ≈ 2.83. Real experiments confirming this won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. When your meter crosses the red line, you've reproduced it — on a simulated pair of qubits, with nothing up anyone's sleeve.
